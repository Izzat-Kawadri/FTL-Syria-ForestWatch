Assessing the Interplay Between Forestry Dynamics and Climate Change in Syria (1990–2025)

An interactive dashboard, built end-to-end with Python, linking CRU temperature & precipitation with FAOSTAT forest land, tree cover, wood production and forest emissions.

1 · Climate problem & research question

Problem statement

Syria has experienced notable shifts in climatic conditions alongside severe environmental pressures and land-use transitions over recent decades. Changes in forest cover and tree-covered areas alter carbon sink capacities and localized climate resilience, while rising temperatures (mean, max and min) and fluctuating precipitation accelerate forest degradation. Understanding the bidirectional relationship between forestry dynamics (forest land cover, tree canopy area, wood production, forest emissions) and climate parameters is vital for regional environmental sustainability.

Main research question

To what extent do historical changes in Syria's forest cover and forestry emissions (1990–2025) correlate with long-term trends in regional temperature and precipitation?

2 · Datasets & Python approach

Data

  • CRU TS 4.10 (via World Bank CCKP API): annual mean / max / min temperature and precipitation, 1901–2025.
  • FAOSTAT Land Use: forest land, planted forest (1000 ha), 1990–2025.
  • FAOSTAT Land Cover: woody crops, tree-covered areas (1000 ha), 1992–2022.
  • FAOSTAT Emissions from Forests: forestland net emissions (kt CO2, FAO Tier 1), 1990–2025.
  • FAOSTAT Forestry Production: roundwood, industrial roundwood, wood fuel, sawlogs, sawnwood, 1961–2024.

Python pipeline (build.py) — with the API authentication fix

  1. Import xlsx/csv with pandas; inspect & clean: filter Syria, pivot items, align on Year.
  2. Live APIs first: World Bank CCKP (both /api/v1 and /cckp/v1 URL shapes) and FAOSTAT.
  3. FAOSTAT JWT fix: the API now rejects anonymous calls. build.py obtains a Bearer token with a free FAOSTAT account (FAOSTAT_USER/FAOSTAT_PASSWORD env vars), caches it 50 min, and tries api/v1 → api/v2.
  4. Resilient fallback chain for every source: live API → cached API copy → local file in data/.
  5. Analyse: mean/min/max, % change, CAGR, linear trend + R²/p, period comparison, rolling means, threshold detection, Pearson correlation + detrended (year-on-year) check.
  6. Visualise & publish: Plotly dashboard + notebook-style figures → static HTML for GitHub Pages.

    9 · Proposed solution & impact

    🛰️ Forest–Climate Monitoring Dashboard

    This open, reproducible dashboard is the first layer: one Python command refreshes every number and chart when new FAOSTAT/CRU releases arrive, so planners can track forest and climate indicators together.

    ⚠️ Early-warning flags

    Threshold detection (drought < 200 mm, heat > 19.5 °C mean) can be automated into annual risk flags that mark years when forests are most exposed.

    🌱 Decision support

    Pair drought-risk years with planting and protection priorities: drought-tolerant species for planted forests and woody crops, fire-risk preparedness after dry, hot years.

    🤖 Next: AI model

    Add satellite tree-cover loss (Global Forest Watch) and fit a regression / random-forest model to predict next-year forest stress from climate indicators.

    Impact

    • Gives researchers, NGOs and local authorities a transparent evidence base for forest and climate-resilience planning.
    • Supports SDG 13 (climate action) and SDG 15 (terrestrial ecosystems).
    • Fully open: code, data links and method are public and reusable for other countries.

    10 · Limitations & next steps

    • Correlation ≠ causation. Trending series (forest area, temperature) correlate by construction; the detrended view is the fairer test.
    • Small sample. ~36 annual points; forest area and emissions in FAOSTAT are modelled (FAO Tier 1) and change in steps or straight lines, not observed annually.
    • National averages. CRU is a country mean; local forest–climate effects (coastal mountains vs steppe) are hidden.
    • Conflict-period data quality. Statistics for 2011 onwards may rely on estimates.
    • Next: sub-national climate data, satellite tree-cover loss, fire data, lagged and multivariate models, forecasting.
